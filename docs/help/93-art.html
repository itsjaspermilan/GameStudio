Drawing pixel art

Your art ends up on a small screen, 320×240 pixels on Dot. Everything on this page serves one goal: the player understands the screen at a glance. How big to draw each thing is on its own page: Sprite and tile sizes. In short: a hero of 32×32 to 48×48, ordinary enemies 24×24 to 32×32, bosses 64×64 to 128×128, pickups 12×12 to 16×16, tiles 16×16 or 32×32.

Colour banks

Colours work the way they do on a GBA. Your game has colour banks (Colours in the side bar, the file palette.json): up to 16 for sprites and 16 for tiles. A bank has 16 places: place 0 is see-through and places 1 to 15 are colours, chosen from the console's 32,768. A sprite draws with one sprite bank; a tileset lists the tile banks it uses and each tile keeps to one of them, so a single tileset can hold grass, water and stone. With every bank in use the screen shows up to 512 colours at once. A new game starts with ready-made banks (hero, knight, villager, slime, fire, ice, ghost, items; meadow, forest, stone, water, desert, castle, station, cave) to change as you like.

Each ready-made bank is three ramps of five shades (places 1-5, 6-10, 11-15), dark to light. That is what makes GBA art look rich instead of flat:

  • Shade with a ramp, not with black. Paint each part from one ramp: the middle shade for its colour, the lighter two where the light falls, the darker two for shadow. Five shades give soft, round forms; four looks hard.
  • Shadows lean cool, highlights lean warm. The ready-made ramps already turn toward blue and purple in the dark and toward yellow in the light, and keep away from pure black and white. Keep that when you pick your own (the Ramp button in Colours makes five such shades from one colour).
  • Outline in a dark colour of the thing itself (place 1 of a ramp), not black, unless it must stand out on a busy background.
  • Muted backgrounds, bright actors. Ground and walls in the calmer tile banks; the hero, enemies and pickups in stronger colours.
  • One bank per kind of character, like on a GBA: the hero has a bank, soldiers have one. A copy of a bank with other colours in the same places is a palette swap: set bank = "red_soldier" on an entity and the same drawing shows in the new colours.
  • Code can use bank colours too: gs.draw.rect(x, y, w, h, "sky:3") is place 3 of the bank sky.

Games made before colour banks keep the console's 32 classic colours (eight families of four shades: grey 0-3, red 4-7, orange and brown 8-11, yellow 12-15, green 16-19, teal 20-23, blue 24-27, purple 28-31) until you open Colours and start banks.

Glow, shadow and see-through

A GBA mixes layers and sprites with what is behind them, and so can your game. Anything that draws (an entity, a tile layer, a picture, text or panel in a layout, gs.draw.sprite, gs.burst) takes a blend and an alpha:

  • blend = "add", glow: adds its light to what is behind. Fire, magic, sparks, a light shaft from a window, a glowing sign. Draw glows in dark-to-bright shades of one colour: black adds nothing, so the edges fade by themselves.
  • blend = "darken", shadow: multiplies, so white changes nothing and darker colours shade. Shadows under trees, tinted glass, a blue layer for night.
  • alpha (0 to 1) is the strength; on its own it makes something see-through: water, ghosts, mist.

In the layout editor, the Look row of a selected picture, text or panel, and of the layer you paint on, sets both. An entity sets them in its file: blend = "add". Code can blend anything it draws: gs.draw.blend("add") ... gs.draw.blend(). Use it for a few things that should catch the eye; a screen where everything glows has nothing that stands out.

Making it readable

  • Shape first. Fill the whole outline in one colour and look at it small. If you can tell what it is, add the details. A character needs a head, a body and feet that are each at least 2 or 3 pixels.
  • Light from the top left, the same on every sprite and tile: lit edges top and left, shadow bottom and right.
  • Colour tells the player who is who. The hero and its shots in cool or bright colours; enemies and their shots in warm reds and oranges; pickups bright and blinking (two frames); the background darker and calmer than anything that moves.
  • Few ramps per sprite. Two or three ramps of a bank (skin, hair, clothes) are plenty; using every colour just looks noisy.
  • Face right. Draw characters facing right; the game mirrors them when they walk left (self.flip).
  • Draw at the real size. Drawing small and enlarging it later makes uneven, blotchy pixels.
  • No stray pixels and no lines that wobble by one pixel; curves are clean steps (1, 2, 3 pixels long, not 1, 3, 1).

For each kind of game

  • Platformer. Side view, 16×16 tiles. The ground needs a clear top edge (a lighter row) so the player sees where to land. Background tiles darker and bluer than the solid ones. Dangers (spikes, lava) in red and orange with sharp shapes.
  • Top-down adventure. Seen a little from the front as well as from above. Characters 24×32 or 32×32, drawn facing down, up and right (left is mirrored), with 2 to 4 walking frames each. Floors quiet, walls taller with a dark line at the bottom.
  • Lots of enemies (survivors, shoot-'em-up). Each kind of enemy different in shape and colour. Enemy bullets bright red or pink with a light centre, so they are the first thing you see; your own shots blue or white and smaller. Effects short and see-through, so they never hide a bullet. A very dark, quiet background.
  • Puzzle. Big flat shapes, one colour per kind of piece, and a symbol on each, so that colour-blind players can play too.
  • Racing, vehicles. For the player's vehicle draw 8 or 16 frames turning; simple shapes can be turned with the sprite's angle instead.

Tilesets

One sheet per place (a cave, a forest), at most 256×256. Tick the tile banks it uses; each tile keeps to one bank, and the editor points out a tile that mixes two (Snap tiles to banks moves each tile to its closest bank). Put the tiles in an order that is easy to remember: plain ground first, then its top edge, the corners, walls, decoration. Make the edges match so the tiles join without seams.
