Working inside the handheld's limits

The handheld is a small, cheap computer. Games made for old consoles (the Game Boy Advance is the model here) worked within tight limits and were better for it: limits make you decide what matters. GameStudio tells you where the limits are and warns you before a game stops fitting.

Consoles

A game is made for a console, named in game.lua. It decides the screen, the palette and the limits. Two come with GameStudio, and they differ only in the screen:

Glow43Glow169
Screen320×240 (4:3)320×180 (16:9)
ColoursGBA-style colour banks: 16 for sprites and 16 for tiles, 15 colours each from 32,768 (up to 512 on screen); older games keep 32 classic colours
Spritesmultiples of 8 pixels, up to 128×128
Tiles8×8, 16×16 or 32×32, sheets up to 512×512
Music / sound effects8 music tracks and 4 sound effect channels at once, with reverb; samples up to 8 seconds at 32 kHz
Level sizeup to 4096×1024 (a long platformer stage in one piece), four tile layers

Change the console with Change console… in the sidebar: it shows what would no longer fit before you commit to it.

Hard limits and budgets

  • Hard limits are enforced. At most 512 entities alive at once: more are ignored, with one warning in the Output. Sprites must be the right sizes and keep to their colour bank.
  • Budgets are soft: a game can go over them, and the Handheld tab shows where it stands. Game size (16 MB, like a GBA cartridge) and frames in a sprite are measured from your files. The console spec also lists 1000 collision checks a step and about six screens of drawing a frame, from tests on the device; the Handheld tab measures both while the game runs (the busiest step and frame of each second), exactly the same as on the device.

The limits are ceilings, not targets. A typical GBA-style game has 20 to 100 things alive and a few hundred collision checks a step; the handheld holds more than that, so you have room for modern touches, but a game that stays near the typical numbers never worries. Particles are entities too, so they count toward the 512: make them short-lived and cap how many you spawn.

The Handheld tab

Under the editor, next to Output. It shows:

  • Size of the game against the budget.
  • Art: your biggest sprite and tileset against what the console allows, frames per sprite, your colour banks against the console's 16 + 16, and any art outside its banks (or, in an older game, outside the classic palette).
  • Sound: how many songs and effects you have, and the most tracks any song uses.
  • While it runs: how many things are alive now and at most, about how long a frame would take on the Brick, the frame rate, and how much of the console's RAM the code uses.

The Play panel shows the last two under the game's screen all the time, for example RAM 0.8 of 8 MB · Brick frame ≈ 2.1 ms (13%). Amber means close to the limit, red means over it.

The studio plays like the Brick. The game's code gets the same 8 MB of RAM as on the handheld (pictures and sounds do not count), so a game that runs out of memory stops with "not enough memory" here, at the same moment it would there. Your computer is many times faster than the handheld, so the time per frame is an estimate: how much the game draws plus how long its update takes here, scaled by measurements on real Bricks. The Brick has 16.7 ms for each frame; keep the estimate well under that (under 70% is comfortable), since a busy moment costs more than an average one. When a game on the device cannot keep up with 60 frames a second, the runtime says so once in its log, with where the time went.

Save state and Load state under the Play panel's screen work like the Brick's own: save just before a hard part, then load to try it again. There is one state, and it lasts until the game stops.

Keeping a game fast

  1. Count what you make. Everything with a lifetime needs an end: bullets leave the screen or time out, effects clean themselves up. Cap enemies with gs.count("enemy") < 150.
  2. Find things by tag. gs.nearest, gs.in_radius, gs.each and gs.count only look at things with that tag, which the engine keeps in a list of their own; collisions use a spatial grid per tag on top. A loop over everything that measures distances is slow.
  3. Let collisions be handled by tags. Writing on_collide_enemy costs nothing until something with that tag is near.
  4. Do not make garbage every step. A new table or closure per entity per step ({ x, y } for a position, a function in update) adds up to a lot of memory churn. Make it once, reuse it, or use two numbers instead. A see-through color can be a string, "#ff000080" (the last two digits: 00 invisible, ff solid), instead of a new { r, g, b, a }. A search every step can refill one list: self.near = gs.in_radius(x, y, 60, "enemy", self.near). The editor points these out in update and draw.
  5. Check collisions less often when you can. A crowd that only needs to push apart does not need a check every step: collide_every = { enemy = 4 } in an entity's table runs that on_collide_enemy every fourth step, spread over the crowd (and you make the push four times as strong). In a survivors game with a hundred enemies this halves the cost of the crowd.
  6. Remember lookups. self.target = gs.first("hero") in create, not every step.
  7. Draw less. Entities off screen are skipped when they use their sprite. Tile layers are drawn from one picture each. Big see-through areas that cover the screen several times are the thing that costs: on the device about six screens' worth of painting in one frame was the most that kept 60 frames a second.
  8. Short update methods. Put the rare, expensive work (path finding, a search) on a timer that runs a few times a second instead of every step.
  9. Use dt. Movement by speed * dt stays the same speed if the frame rate dips.

How inheritance affects speed: when an entity builds on another (gs.entity(Parent, { ... })), the engine copies the parent's methods into the child when the file loads, so calling a method is one lookup however long the family tree is.
