Music and sound effects

A player hears the same song for minutes and the same sound effect hundreds of times. Make both pleasant first, exciting second. How to use the sound and music editors is on Sprites, animation and sound.

Instruments

There are two families. The chip sounds (lead, pluck, flute, bell and the like) are tiny retro waveforms: great for a retro game, but harsh and whistly as a melody. The General MIDI instruments (their names start with gm_) are real recorded instruments: pianos, guitars, strings, brass, pads, a drum kit. Use them unless you want the retro sound on purpose.

Keeping it pleasant

  • Keep the melody in the middle. Between C4 and C6. Above C6 most instruments turn thin and shrill; above C7 they pierce.
  • Accompany below the tune. Chords and arpeggios an octave under the melody, the bass between C1 and C3.
  • Land on the chords. On the strong beats (1 and 3) let the melody play a note of the chord under it; move between them mostly by step.
  • Stay in one range. A tune you can hum stays within about an octave and a half and has few big jumps.
  • Repeat a little. A phrase that comes back is what makes a tune stick. Two to four chords that return work well.
  • Even loudness. Every song about as loud as the others, so moving between screens never jumps in volume.

Short (under half a second), one clear idea each, and different for each kind of event so the player hears what happened.

The sounds you hear most (shots, footsteps) should be the quietest: they repeat hundreds of times.

Eight tracks and reverb

A song has eight tracks. Use them the way GBA composers did: a lead, a counter-melody or harmony, a pad or strings holding the chords, a bass, and three or four for drums (kick, snare, hi-hat, a crash or percussion). More parts are not more noise: keep each in its own range and give the quiet ones a lower track volume.

Reverb puts the music in a space. Room (about 10 to 25) is the safe choice for most levels and menus; Hall suits title screens, castles and calm, slow songs; Cave suits caves, ruins and anything eerie. Fast songs want less reverb than slow ones (it blurs quick notes), and drums sound tighter with little. Sound effects stay dry, except the few that belong in the space: a spell, a bell, a door, a voice.

On the handheld

A four-track General MIDI song takes about a tenth of the handheld's processor time on its own thread; eight tracks of General MIDI take about twice that (not yet measured on the device). Chip instruments cost much less, so they are good for drums and for the extra parts. Do not play many General MIDI sound effects at once.

With an agent

An AI agent connected to GameStudio follows this page with its tools: instruments lists what exists; song_write writes a song (from a short description of key, tempo, band and chords, or note by note); song_check reads the notes like a musician (key, chords, whether the melody sits on them, clashing notes, shrill or muddy ranges) and measures the sound (loudness, clipping, brightness); sound_write and sound_check do the same for effects.
